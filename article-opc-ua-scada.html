The case for OPC UA in modern water utility SCADA architectures

02 May 2026
8 min read
By Eng. Sarah Okonkwo
For two decades, water utility SCADA architectures across MENA have been held together by Modbus RTU, IEC 60870-5-104, and a generous helping of proprietary protocols. That worked when SCADA was a centralized monitoring tool. It doesn't work when SCADA has to become the operational backbone of a 14,000-tag, multi-site water utility.

When we modernized the Greater Cairo Water Authority SCADA last year — 42 pumping stations, six treatment plants, 11 million people served — we made a deliberate architectural decision early in the project. The new master SCADA would talk to every field PLC over OPC UA, with IEC 60870-5-104 retained only for the legacy dispatch interface.

That decision was not popular at first. OPC UA has a reputation for being heavy, complex, and overkill for "simple" SCADA needs. After 18 months of live operation, here's what we've actually learned.

Why legacy protocols stop scaling

Modbus, IEC-104, and DNP3 share a common limitation: they're flat, register-based, and carry no semantic context. A register at address 40100 might be a pressure reading in bar — or kPa, or psi — and you'd never know from the protocol itself. That metadata lives in spreadsheets, tribal knowledge, and engineering drawings that nobody updates.

At small scale this is annoying. At 14,000 tags across 48 sites, it becomes a daily operational risk. We've seen utilities run for years with wrong unit assumptions in their dispatch — the data was being read correctly, but interpreted incorrectly.

What OPC UA actually delivers

OPC UA solves three structural problems that water utility SCADA has been quietly suffering from:

  • Information modeling. Tags carry their own units, descriptions, types, and engineering bounds. The master SCADA discovers the field PLC's tag structure automatically — no spreadsheet, no manual mapping.
  • Built-in security. X.509 certificate-based authentication and per-session encryption are part of the protocol, not bolted on. For critical infrastructure this is no longer optional.
  • Native scalability. Subscriptions, publish-on-change, and proper namespace structure let a master SCADA monitor 14,000 tags with a fraction of the network traffic that polled IEC-104 would require.
The unspoken benefit of OPC UA is operational: when a new pumping station gets commissioned, integrating it into central SCADA is a half-day job, not a two-week engineering exercise.

The migration playbook

The biggest mistake utilities make in OPC UA migrations is trying to do it all at once. Our recommended approach, refined over three large deployments:

  1. Start with one greenfield site. Use it to build your namespace conventions, certificate management, and operational runbooks.
  2. Migrate one critical site (typically a treatment plant). This validates the architecture under real load.
  3. Roll out remaining sites in parallel waves. Each wave should include a brownfield site retrofit so the team builds that muscle.

The first two phases take about 40% of the calendar time but eliminate 80% of the integration risk. Skipping them is the most common reason OPC UA migrations stall.

Measured outcomes

On Greater Cairo Water Authority, the post-migration numbers spoke for themselves: 23% reduction in pumping energy costs through smarter scheduling enabled by tag-rich SCADA data, mean time to detect pipe-burst events down from 4 hours to 12 minutes, and a unified central control room that consolidates what used to be three separate operational silos.

OPC UA isn't a silver bullet — but for any utility scaling past a few hundred field tags, it's no longer the harder choice. It's the easier one.
