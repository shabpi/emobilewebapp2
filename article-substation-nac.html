Substation automation: lessons from the New Administrative Capital

05 April 2026
9 min read
By Dr. Marcus Reinhardt
Greenfield substation projects look simple on a one-line diagram. They aren't. The 66kV automation deployment we delivered for the New Administrative Capital this year carries operational lessons that apply to every modern substation built in the region — and a few that may surprise even experienced power engineers.

From the outside, the New Administrative Capital substation is a routine 12-bay 66/22kV facility. From the inside, it represented one of the first MENA deployments of a fully IEC 61850-native protection and control architecture coupled with bay-level GOOSE messaging, station-level MMS, and direct integration into the national dispatch via IEC 60870-5-104.

That sentence packs in three standards and three architectural decisions that each carried their own design tradeoffs. Here's what we learned.

IEC 61850 is not plug-and-play

The IEC 61850 marketing materials suggest a world where IEDs from any vendor interoperate seamlessly through SCL files. The reality on a 36-IED deployment is more nuanced. Inter-vendor GOOSE works — but only if you've done your SCL engineering carefully, your network topology supports the multicast load, and your IEDs are all on firmware versions that have been tested together.

On NAC we ran three rounds of IED-to-IED interoperability testing during FAT, two of which surfaced issues that wouldn't have appeared in single-vendor commissioning. The third round caught zero issues — and that's the round you want to budget for.

Process bus is the right answer (eventually)

One of the architectural debates on this project was whether to deploy a conventional copper-wired CT/VT scheme or to move to a full IEC 61850-9-2 process bus with merging units and sampled values. We chose the conventional approach for this build — but the substation's network infrastructure was designed to allow a process bus retrofit in 5–7 years.

The cost of being process-bus-ready at design time is trivial. The cost of retrofitting a substation that wasn't designed for it is enormous. Future-proof the network, even if you don't commit to the topology today.

The protection coordination problem

Modern substations have more protection functions, more communication paths, and more software complexity than ever. The unavoidable consequence is that protection coordination — the discipline of making sure relays trip in the right sequence — has become a multi-disciplinary exercise.

On NAC we ran the protection coordination study three times:

  1. Initial coordination based on design data and standard time-overcurrent settings.
  2. Refined coordination after IED-specific testing, including instantaneous and pickup behavior measurements.
  3. Final coordination after live system tests, with adjustments for the actual measured system impedance.

Each round changed settings. The third round caught a misgrade that would have caused a transformer to trip on a downstream feeder fault.

Dispatch integration: don't outsource the boring part

The integration with national dispatch over IEC 60870-5-104 is, on paper, the simplest part of a substation automation project. In practice it's where many projects burn weeks they didn't budget for. The protocol itself is straightforward — what's not straightforward is the dispatcher's data model, their alarm philosophy, their command authorization rules, and their cyber-security posture.

We've now done enough of these to bake a two-week "dispatcher engagement window" into every substation automation schedule. It's the cheapest insurance you can buy against project delay.

What we'd do differently

If we re-engineered NAC tomorrow, two things would change. We'd specify dual redundant station-level networks from day one rather than rely on RSTP recovery — RSTP works, but PRP/HSR works without the recovery time. And we'd build a dedicated cyber-security review into the protection design phase, not bolt it on at commissioning.

Substation automation is one of those disciplines where every project teaches you something. NAC taught us a lot. The next one will teach us more.
