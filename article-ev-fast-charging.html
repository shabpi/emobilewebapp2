Scaling EV fast-charging across Egypt's national highway corridors

18 May 2026
7 min read
By Eng. Mahmoud Hassan
Egypt's electric vehicle adoption is no longer a question of "if" — it's a question of "how fast." But for EV adoption to scale beyond Cairo and Alexandria, the country needs something we haven't talked about enough: a national fast-charging backbone designed by engineers, not retrofitted by retailers.

When we delivered the Cairo–Alexandria EV corridor in 2025 — 18 ultra-fast stations across 220 kilometres of highway — we learned that scaling EV charging infrastructure across a national geography is a fundamentally different problem from rolling out chargers in shopping mall parking lots.

The challenges are structural: limited medium-voltage grid availability along desert highways, station-to-station distance constraints driven by real-world EV battery anxiety, harsh thermal environments that punish poorly-designed power electronics, and the operational reality that field maintenance teams need to be able to reach a faulted station within four hours.

The 90-kilometre rule

One of the first design decisions on the Cairo–Alex corridor was station spacing. Based on real-world WLTP-derated range data across the popular EVs in the Egyptian market, we settled on a maximum 90-kilometre gap between stations. That number isn't theoretical — it's the practical limit at which drivers stop experiencing range anxiety and start treating EV travel as routine.

That single decision drove site selection across the entire corridor and pushed us to engineer power supply solutions for locations that the existing utility grid wasn't designed to support.

Power isn't the problem people think it is

The most common misconception about national-scale EV charging is that the chargers are the hard part. They aren't. Modern 180kW DC fast chargers are commodity hardware. The hard part is the medium-voltage civil work, the utility coordination, and the smart load management that lets a four-stall ultra-fast station operate within a constrained grid envelope.

On the Cairo–Alex corridor, three of our 18 stations required brand-new 22kV feeder runs — one of them six kilometres long. That single feeder cost more than the chargers themselves.

The operational reality

Where most EV charging conversations stop at "we installed the chargers," ours started there. Within six months of commissioning, we'd already handled 4,200+ active session users and learned three operational lessons:

  • Cellular backhaul is non-negotiable. Wi-Fi-tethered chargers fail in ways that are invisible until a customer complains. All 18 stations run dual-SIM 4G/5G uplinks.
  • Payment integration eats engineering hours. Visa is straightforward. Mada and Fawry are not. We engineered the integrations ourselves and they're now a reusable module for future deployments.
  • Predictive maintenance pays for itself. Our OCPP backend flags charger anomalies — cooling fan vibration, contactor wear, communication drift — 7–14 days before they become failures.

What comes next

The next 60 stations are already in engineering. Cairo–Hurghada, Cairo–Suez, Cairo–Aswan along the Nile, and the start of a GCC-wide harmonization initiative with our partners in Saudi Arabia and the UAE.

The opportunity in front of us isn't to install EV chargers. It's to build the operational infrastructure that makes Egypt's transition to electric mobility actually work — at national scale, for decades, in conditions where reliability is non-negotiable.
